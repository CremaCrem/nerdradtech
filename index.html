Overview

1.1

Definition and basic concepts

Computed tomography produces a 3D digital tomographic image from diagnostic x-rays. "Tomo" means slice. CT slices the patient on a computer instead of with a knife.

  1. InputRadiation + anatomyx-rays pass through the patient
  2. MeasuredProjectionsmany views from many angles
  3. Output2D, 3D or virtual imagestopogram and MPR · SSD and SVD · virtual colonoscopy

How a CT scan works, in plain words

  1. The patient lies on a couch that slides into the gantry, the big ring.
  2. Inside the ring an x-ray tube spins around the patient. Across from it, detectors measure how much x-ray made it through.
  3. Each thin line of x-rays is a ray. Bone weakens it a lot, air hardly at all. That weakening is attenuation.
  4. All rays from one angle make one projection. Hundreds of angles together are the raw data: numbers, not a picture yet.
  5. A computer runs a reconstruction algorithm that turns the raw data into a matrix of pixels, each holding a CT number.
  6. The image is displayed, adjusted (windowing, 3D), stored and sent to wherever it is needed.

Board points

  • Produces a 3D digital tomographic image from diagnostic x-rays
  • Usually performed transversely, giving a high-quality direct transverse image
  • Direct coronal images are possible by positioning the patient and tilting the gantry
  • Consists of a finely-collimated beam and a detector
  • 2D images: topogram, MPR · 3D: SVD, SSD · Virtual: virtual colonoscopy
Lab

The raisin bread analogy

The lecture's picture: the patient's body is the whole loaf, the skin is the brown crust, internal organs are the white bread and a raisin is the pathology. A plain x-ray squashes the whole loaf into one shadow, so raisins pile on top of each other. CT cuts the loaf into slices and shows each one on its own. Slide the knife along the loaf.

Direct transverse vs direct coronal

Transverse is the default. A direct coronal image (sinuses, for example) needs the head tipped back and the gantry tilted, so the beam cuts the anatomy in the coronal plane.

Evolution of terms

  2. Computer-assisted tomography or computerized axial tomography (CAT)

  4. Computerized tomography

1.4

Image manipulation: gray scale (windowing)

Image manipulation is digital processing that makes images more useful for diagnosis: image smoothing, edge enhancement, gray scale manipulation and 3D image processing (such as MIP and minimum intensity projection, which keeps the lowest values and so shows air-filled airways).

Gray scale manipulation is the one you calculate on the board. A CT image holds about 4,000 HU values, but a monitor shows only a few hundred grays. The window width (WW) is how many HU are spread across the grays, and the window level (WL) is the HU at the middle gray.

Prof's tip

Wide WW = long scale of contrast, more grays, lower contrast (lung, bone). Narrow WW = short scale, high contrast (brain, soft tissue). WL should sit near the HU of the tissue you want to see.

2.2

Pixel, voxel and matrix

Pixel

2D

Picture element: each cell of information on the flat image.

  • Large pixel → poor spatial resolution
  • Small pixel → better spatial resolution

Voxel

3D

Volume element: the pixel plus the slice thickness, so a small block of the patient.

  • Large voxel → poor spatial resolution
  • Small voxel → better spatial resolution

Matrix

#

The grid of rows and columns of pixels.

  • Original EMI: 80 × 80
  • Most common: 512 × 512
  • Large matrix → better spatial resolution
Lab

Partial volume averaging

One big pixel can't show four different tissues. It stores their average. The lecture's example: +500, −200, +60 and +40 inside one pixel.

Lab

Fixed FOV

Prof's tip beyond the slides

Pixel size = FOV ÷ matrix. A 25 cm FOV on a 512 matrix: 250 mm ÷ 512 ≈ 0.49 mm. Voxel volume = pixel size² × slice thickness. Convert cm to mm first. This calculation shows up on boards a lot.

2.3

Field of view (FOV)

FOV

The diameter of image reconstruction. Unit: cm. Two types: display FOV and scan FOV.

Display FOV (DFOV)

  • Target or zoom region
  • The section of data selected for display on the image
  • Cannot be larger than the SFOV

Scan FOV (SFOV)

  • Also called the calibration FOV
  • The area within the gantry from which raw data for calibration is acquired
  • Anything outside the SFOV is not imaged → out-of-field artifact
2.4

Beam attenuation and the linear attenuation coefficient

Beam attenuation is the reduction in the intensity of a radiation beam as it passes through an object. Four things decide how much: tissue mass density, tissue thickness, atomic number and radiation energy.

Lab

Send photons through tissue

Tissue (density and atomic number)

Energy

µ (cm⁻¹)
Transmitted

Teaching values, rounded. Water is set to the lecture's 0.18 cm⁻¹.

Linear attenuation coefficient (µ)

The amount of the x-ray beam that is scattered or absorbed per unit of material (the slide says "per unit mass"; its unit is cm⁻¹, meaning per centimetre).

Water: µ = 0.18 cm⁻¹ at 125 kVp → about 18% of the beam absorbed or scattered per centimetre.

Prof's tip

Bone attenuates more than water because it is denser and has a higher atomic number (calcium). A higher kVp makes every tissue attenuate less.

2.5

Hounsfield units (HU)

Also called CT numbers or density values: the number stored in each pixel. It measures how much a structure attenuates the beam compared with water, and is used to judge what kind of tissue it is. Move along the scale.

Point at the scale.

Calculate a CT number beyond the slides

HU = 1000 × (µtissue − µwater) ÷ µwater

Board points

  • Range on the slides: −1000 to +3000
  • Negative: attenuates less than water (fat, lung, air)
  • Positive: attenuates more than water (blood, organs, bone)
  • Water = 0, air = −1000 by definition
2.6

Ray, projection and translation

Lab

Measure a 3 × 3 patient

Ray

The path the x-ray beam takes from the tube to the detector.

Ray sum: how much of the beam was attenuated along that ray.

Projection / view

The attenuation (intensity) profile: a set of rays striking a detector array. A complete set of ray sums.

Translation

A sweep or scan: the movement of the source-detector assembly across the patient. It results in one projection (view).

3.1

Basic scheme and scanner generations

Basic scheme for data acquisition

  • The first step of the CT process
  • The method by which the patient is scanned to obtain enough data for image reconstruction
  • Involves the gantry and the patient couch

Scanner generation

Decided by how the x-ray tube is arranged relative to the detectors, based on:

  • Scanning geometry (parallel, fan or cone beam)
  • Scanning motion (translate, rotate, stationary)
  • Number of detectors

There are 1st to 7th generations.

Lab

Watch each generation move

Prof's tip

Remember the motions: 1st and 2nd translate–rotate, 3rd rotate–rotate, 4th rotate–stationary, 5th stationary–stationary (nothing moves). In the 3rd generation the beam's apex is at the tube. In the 4th it is at the detector.

3.2

Helical CT and pitch

Helical (spiral) CT

  • Continuous acquisition scanning
  • Continuously rotating x-ray tube with constant output
  • Uninterrupted table movement
  • Volumetric scanning
  • Reconstruct at any z-axis position (interpolated)
  • Made possible by slip-ring technology

Advantages

  • Increased speed
  • Improved image resolution
  • Better iodinated contrast concentration in images
  • Fewer motion artifacts
  • Needs less contrast media
  • Superior MPR and 3D reformation
  • More patient comfort, more department productivity

Disadvantages

  • A full 360° set of projection data is not acquired for each section
  • The data is slanted, so it has to be interpolated
  • Slightly poorer resolution than step-and-shoot

3.3

Seventh generation: multislice CT

Multislice or multidetector CT (MSCT/MDCT) has many detector rows side by side along z, so it produces multiple slices per gantry rotation. Single-detector CT uses a thin fan beam. MDCT opens the beam into a cone beam. Spatial resolution is decided by the width of the detector, not by the beam.

Lab

One 20 mm beam, three ways to slice it

MSCT advantages

  • Longer and faster z-axis coverage per gantry rotation
  • More volume covered per unit time
  • Allows post-acquisition reconstruction
  • Increases image resolution
  • Adjustable slice thickness
  • Lecture example: 20 mm collimated beam, four 5 mm detectors → four 5 mm, two 10 mm, or one 20 mm section

✦
  • Produces a heterogeneous (polychromatic) beam

▽

  • Bowtie filter: shapes the energy distribution across the beam for uniform beam hardening

▭

  • Moves vertically, horizontally, both, or automatically
  • Emergency pull stops the table in an emergency or malfunction

  • Scannable range: how far the table moves horizontally, meaning how much can be scanned without repositioning

3.6

Collimation

CT has two collimators, and together they affect both patient dose and image quality. Open and close each one.

Pre-patient collimator

  • a.k.a. source collimator
  • Between the x-ray tube and the patient
  • Limits the area of the patient in the useful beam
  • Determines patient dose (dose profile)

Post-patient collimator

  • a.k.a. predetector collimator
  • Below the patient, above the detector array
  • Restricts the beam the detectors see · decreases scatter · improves contrast
  • Determines slice thickness (sensitivity profile)
3.7

Detectors

Detectors capture the radiation beam coming out of the patient, convert it into electrical signals, which are then turned into binary coded data. Eight characteristics describe a good one.

EfficiencyCapture, absorb and convert photons (lab below)
Response timeDetect one event and recover for the next. Very short (ms), to avoid afterglow and "pile-up"
Dynamic rangeLargest signal ÷ smallest signal it can tell apart: 1,000,000 : 1
StabilitySteadiness of response. Unstable systems need frequent calibration. Gas detectors are more stable
AfterglowThe image persists after the beam stops. CT must be low: < 0.01% at 100 ms, subtracted before reconstruction
ResolutionSet by element size, focal spot size, sampling (detector pitch) and crosstalk. Higher with small detectors, small FSS, low pitch and spacing bars
CrosstalkSignal leaking into the neighbouring element. Causes noise and lowers resolution. Reduced by spacing bars, small gaps, pre/post and septal collimators
AccuracyMeasures the true transmitted intensity
Lab

Detector designer: follow 1,000 photons

Material

Capture efficiency

(total detected photons ÷ total incident photons) × 100

Affected by the size of the detector area facing the beam and the distance between two detectors. High capture → more reliable data.

Absorption efficiency

The number of photons absorbed by the detector. Depends on its atomic number, physical density, size and face thickness. High → low dose, low noise, high resolution.

Conversion efficiency

(total electrical signals ÷ total incident photons) × 100

High → high SNR, less grainy image, low dose, faster scan, better contrast and resolution.

Placement, size, spacing

  • Placement: as close together as possible, so all x-rays become data
  • Size and shape: deep, narrow accepts less scatter (desirable). Short, wide accepts more (undesirable)
  • Spacing: measured from the middle of one detector to the middle of the next
  • Aperture: the size of the opening. Small aperture = good spatial resolution and good scatter rejection
  • Spacing bars separate detectors, let them sit in an arc or circle, and prevent crosstalk

4.3

Back projection

The scanner only knows ray sums. Back projection works backwards: it spreads each sum back along its ray and adds all the spreads. This is the lecture's worked example: a 2 × 2 object holding 1, 2, 3, 4, measured from four directions.

Board points

  • a.k.a. summation or linear superposition method
  • Oldendorf (1961) and Kuhl and Edwards (1963)
  • Converts data from the attenuation profile into a matrix
  • Sums all back-projected images to form the CT image
  • Not sharp · not used in clinical CT
  • Artifact: the typical star pattern
  • One back projection reveals little. Many back projections resemble the object
  • 2 × 2 answer: (BP + P1 + P2 + P3 − 10) ÷ 3
4.4

Convolution

A convolution filter changes an attenuation profile before it is back projected, like a photo filter that smooths or sharpens. Pick one and compare it with the dashed original.

Smoothing filterEdge-enhancing filter
Reduces the difference between adjacent pixelsAccentuates the difference between neighbouring pixels
Low contrast resolution, low spatial resolutionHigh spatial resolution, high contrast resolution
Image smoothing: soft tissueEdge enhancement: bone
Low noise, more blurredLow blurring, high noise

Board points

  • A digital image processing technique
  • Applies a filter to an attenuation profile, changing pixel values by a formula
  • Used to minimize streak artifacts
  • Applied only to raw data, not image data
  • Deconvolution: returns pixel values to their original level by a reverse process

Memory trick: smooth = soft tissue, sharp = skeleton.

4.5

Filtered back projection

Convolution+Back projection=Filtered back projection

One small dot is scanned. Left: plain back projection, where every smear runs right across, so spokes stick out. Right: each profile is filtered first. The filter adds small negative values beside each edge that cancel the spokes. Slide the number of projections.

Board points

  • a.k.a. the convolution method: the projection profile is convolved (filtered)
  • Advantage: removes the star-like blurring of simple back projection
  • Disadvantage: noise and streak artifacts
4.6

Fourier transformation splits one complicated wave into simple waves of different frequencies, and can add them back to rebuild the original. The lecture compares it to hearing. Toggle the waves, then listen.

4.7

Helical interpolation

In helical scanning the tube draws a spiral, so no flat slice is ever measured. Interpolation estimates the missing value from known values on either side. Below the spiral is unrolled: across is the tube angle, up is table position. For each angle the scanner takes the measurement just below the slice and the one a full 360° later just above it: 360° linear interpolation.

Board points

  • A mathematical method of creating missing data
  • Estimates an unknown value from known values
  • Linear interpolation: the unknown point falls on a straight line between two known points (A = 5, C = 15 → B = 10)
  • Used to straighten up the sectional image
  • A post-acquisition algorithm applied to the data set
  • 1st algorithm: 360° linear interpolation. Disadvantage: prominent blurring
4.8

Iterative reconstruction

Iterative = repeated. It is guess and check: start with every box the same value, work out what the scanner would have measured, compare it with what it did measure, correct, repeat. Same 1, 2, 3, 4 object.

Board points

  • Starts by assuming all points in the matrix have the same value
  • Performed after obtaining all projection data sets
  • Advantages: reduced image noise (primary), low patient dose (primary), better CNR, minimal streaks (metal, motion, photon starvation)
  • Disadvantages: needs GPUs, computationally intensive, slower reconstruction

Prof's tip

Magnification vs decreasing the DFOV is a favourite trap. Magnification enlarges the existing pixels (no new detail). Decreasing the DFOV is a retrospective reconstruction from raw data, giving smaller pixels and real detail. Compare the two tools above.

6.1

Criteria for evaluating a CT image

SPATIAL RESOLUTION

How small?

How well small objects and sharp edges show as separate things.

LOW-CONTRAST RESOLUTION

How different?

How well tissues with only slightly different densities can be told apart. CT's strength.

TEMPORAL RESOLUTION

How fast?

How well moving anatomy (the heart, a contrast bolus) is frozen in time.

6.2

Factors that raise each one

High spatial resolution

  • Thin slice thickness
  • Small pixel size
  • Small detector aperture
  • Small voxel size
  • Large matrix size
  • Small DFOV (fixed matrix)
  • Bone tissue filter (reconstruction kernel)
  • Low pitch
  • Short scan time
  • Small focal spot size

High low-contrast resolution

  • Increase mAs (dose)
  • Increase SNR
  • Large pixel size
  • Thick slice thickness
  • Soft tissue filter (reconstruction kernel)
  • Small patient size
  • Wide window width (long scale of contrast)

High temporal resolution

  • Fast gantry rotation
  • Scan mode: helical, MSCT, EBCT, dual source
  • Short exposure time
  • Cardiac gating
  • Improved contrast timing
  • Minimized patient motion

The uncoupling effect

In digital CT the image is not directly linked to the dose: more dose doesn't make the picture darker or lighter like film did, so you can't "see" overexposure. Change the mAs above: brightness stays the same and only the noise changes.

  • When changing the radiation dose, manipulate mAs rather than kVp
  • mAs is more flexible and directly affects image quality (noise)

Last-minute summary

Only the facts the board asks about. Read it the night before, or print it (it prints with the glossary).

IDefinition

  • 3D digital tomographic image from diagnostic x-rays
  • Usually transverse · finely collimated beam + detector
  • Raisin bread: loaf = body, crust = skin, white = organs, raisin = pathology
  • Today's term: computed tomography

IAdvantages vs disadvantages

  • + Good low-contrast resolution, WW/WL, volume data (helical), 3D/MPR, bone, large vessels (320/370 mgI/mL)
  • − Worse spatial resolution: 1 lp/mm (SF 7, DR 5), high dose, artifacts, contrast allergy

ICT processes

  • Acquisition (get data) → Reconstruction (use data) → Display (display data) → Manipulation → Storage → Communication
  • Prospective = during scan · Retrospective = same raw data later
  • 1st gen: translate–rotate, 1°, 180 translations
  • DICOM · PACS–RIS–HIS

IWindowing

  • Upper = WL + WW/2 · Lower = WL − WW/2
  • Bone 2000/300 → −700 to 1300
  • Lung 1500/−500 → −1250 to 250
  • Below = black · above = white

IISlice and pixels

  • Z = thickness · X = width · Y = height
  • X miscentering: patient to one side · Y: table too high/low
  • Pixel 2D · voxel 3D · matrix: EMI 80×80, common 512×512
  • Partial volume: (+500 −200 +60 +40)/4 = +100
  • Pixel = FOV ÷ matrix

IIFOV, µ, HU

  • DFOV = zoom, ≤ SFOV · SFOV = calibration FOV
  • Attenuation: density, thickness, Z, energy
  • µ water 0.18 cm⁻¹ @125 kVp → 18%/cm
  • HU range −1000 to +3000, water 0
  • Ray → ray sum · projection = set of ray sums · translation = one projection

IIIGenerations

  • 1: TR, parallel, EMI, 80×80, water bag
  • 2: TR, fan, whole body, bowtie
  • 3: RR, curvilinear 30–60°, most used, apex at tube
  • 4: R-stationary ring, overscan 400°, apex at detector
  • 5: EBCT, 33/50/100 ms
  • 6: helical · dual source 90°
  • 7: MSCT, cone beam

IIIHelical, pitch, MSCT

  • Slip rings made helical possible
  • Pitch = couch movement/rotation ÷ beam width
  • Low pitch: slow, high dose, high res, oversampling
  • High pitch: fast, low dose, low res, undersampling
  • 20 mm = 4×5 / 2×10 / 1×20 mm

IIIHardware

  • Aperture 70 cm · tilt ±30° (0.5° steps)
  • HF generator inside gantry, 20–100 kW (120 kW)
  • Anode ≥5 in., 12°, 3,600–10,000 rpm · Straton 5 MHU/min
  • Pre-patient = dose · post-patient = slice thickness, scatter
  • Couch: carbon fiber, 220 kg

IIIDetectors

  • SID 44 in. · dynamic range 1,000,000:1
  • Afterglow < 0.01% at 100 ms
  • Xenon: 60–90%, no afterglow, stable, directional, not in 4th gen/helical
  • Scintillation: ~100%, afterglow, photodiode, all helical/MSCT
  • Deep narrow detector = less scatter

IVReconstruction basics

  • Microprocessor 30 s · array processor <1 s
  • Single source 180° · dual source 90°
  • MIP: highest value, simplest, CTA
  • SSD: narrow range, bone, virtual colonoscopy
  • SVD: very sensitive to pixel range

IVMath algorithms

  • BP: summation, Oldendorf 1961, star pattern, not clinical
  • Convolution: raw data only, minimizes streaks
  • FBP = convolution + BP · noise & streaks
  • Fourier: MRI, hearing
  • Helical interp.: 360° linear → blurring
  • Iterative: low dose & noise, needs GPUs

VDisplay

  • Last phase · DAC
  • Scanogram = localizer, scout, surview, topogram, preview, pilot
  • Magnification ≠ smaller FOV, no better resolution
  • ROI mostly circular · SD = variance in ROI
  • Multiple images = saves film

VIImage quality

  • Spatial: thin slice, small pixel/voxel/aperture/FSS, large matrix, bone kernel, low pitch
  • Contrast: ↑mAs, thick slice, large pixel, soft kernel, small patient, wide WW
  • Temporal: fast rotation, helical/MSCT/EBCT/dual source, gating
  • Uncoupling: change mAs, not kVp

VIArtifacts

  • Physics: beam hardening (dark bands, petrous) · cupping · partial volume · aliasing (fine stripes) · edge gradient (barium–air)
  • Patient: motion (ghosting) · metal (streaks) · out-of-field (arms down)
  • Equipment: ring (detector, 3rd gen) · tube arcing · spiral interpolation · cone beam (windmill)
Appendix

Words to know

Every dotted word on the page links here. Plain-language definitions first, board wording in the chapters.

The scanner

Dotted-underline word
Like this one. Tap any of them to see what it means.
CT (computed tomography)
An x-ray exam where a computer builds cross-section pictures ("slices") of the body from many x-ray measurements taken around the patient.
Gantry
The ring-shaped part of the scanner. The x-ray tube and detectors spin around inside it.
X-ray tube
The part that produces the x-ray beam. It circles the patient during the scan.
Detector
Sits opposite the tube and measures how much x-ray came out the other side of the patient.
Isocenter
The exact center of the gantry, where the tube rotates around. The patient's center should sit here.
Positioning lasers
Red light lines from the gantry that cross at the isocenter, used to center the patient.
Helical (spiral) scanning
Scanning while the table keeps moving, so the tube's path around the patient becomes a spiral (a helix).
Dose
How much radiation the patient receives. Lower dose is safer but the image gets grainier (more noise).

Data and images

Attenuation
How much the x-ray beam is weakened as it passes through something. Bone attenuates a lot, air almost nothing.
Ray
One thin line of x-rays going from the tube, through the patient, to one detector element.
Ray sum
The total attenuation along one ray. The scanner only ever knows these totals, never the values inside.
Attenuation profile (projection)
All the ray sums measured from one angle, side by side: a "shadow" of the body from that direction.
Raw data
The measurements straight from the detectors, before any image exists.
Sinogram
A picture of the raw data: every attenuation profile stacked as one row per angle. It looks like wavy stripes, not like a body.
Image data
The finished picture after reconstruction: pixels with CT numbers.
Algorithm
A set of step-by-step rules for getting a specific output from a specific input. Like a recipe, but for a computer.
Matrix
The grid of rows and columns that makes up the image, for example 512 × 512 boxes.
Pixel
One box in the matrix (2D, flat).
Voxel
A pixel with thickness: one tiny block of the patient's tissue.
CT number / Hounsfield unit (HU)
The number in each pixel that tells how much that tissue attenuates. Water = 0, air = −1000, bone about +400 to +1000 or more.
Window width (WW)
How many HU are spread across the gray scale. Wide = many grays, low contrast. Narrow = few grays, high contrast.
Window level (WL)
The HU value shown as middle gray: the center of the window.
Operator-selected pixel range
A range of CT numbers chosen by the person running the scanner, for example 200 to 1200 HU. Only values inside the range are used.
Post-acquisition
Done after the scan is finished, using data already collected.

3D, exams and quality

MPR (multiplanar reformation)
Re-arranging the stack of slices to view the body in other directions or in 3D, without scanning again.
Volume rendering
Showing the whole 3D block at once, partly see-through, so it looks like a real 3D object.
Contrast medium
A dye (usually iodine) injected so blood vessels and some organs show up bright.
CT angiography (CTA)
A CT scan of blood vessels, done with contrast medium.
Virtual colonoscopy
Using CT to "fly through" the inside of the colon on screen.
Artifact
Anything on the image that is not really in the patient.
Star pattern
The artifact of simple back projection: spokes or a halo around bright objects.
Streak artifact
Bright or dark lines across the image, often from metal, motion or too little x-ray.
Noise
A grainy, speckled look. Worse at low dose or when edges are sharpened.
Spatial resolution
How well the image shows tiny details and sharp edges as separate things.
Contrast resolution
How well the image shows tissues that are only slightly different in density.
CNR (contrast-to-noise ratio)
How clearly a structure stands out compared with the graininess around it. Higher is better.
Photon starvation
Too few x-rays reach the detector (for example through both shoulders), causing streaks.

Processing words

Filter (kernel)
A math formula that changes the numbers in a profile, either to smooth them or to sharpen edges.
Convolution
Applying a filter to the raw data. Each value is changed based on its neighbours.
Deconvolution
The reverse process: returning pixel values to their original level.
Back projection
Spreading each ray sum back across the image along the path it came from, then adding everything up.
Interpolation
Estimating a missing value from the known values on either side of it.
Iteration
One round of a repeat. Iterative = done again and again, getting a bit better each time.
Fourier transformation
A math method that splits one complicated wave into simple waves of different frequencies.
Frequency
How fast a wave goes up and down. A high-frequency sound is a high-pitched sound.
GPU
A powerful computer chip built for heavy math. Iterative reconstruction needs it.
