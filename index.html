How a CT scan works, in plain words

  1. The patient lies on a table that slides into the gantry, the big ring-shaped part of the scanner.
  2. Inside the ring, an x-ray tube spins around the patient. Directly across from it, detectors measure how much x-ray made it through the body.
  3. Every thin line of x-rays from the tube to the detector is called a ray. Dense things like bone block more x-ray than soft things like fat, so less reaches the detector. This weakening of the beam is called attenuation.
  4. All the rays measured from one angle make one attenuation profile (also called a projection). It is a row of numbers, like a shadow of the body. The tube collects hundreds of these from different angles.
  5. Together, all these measurements are the raw data. They are numbers, not a picture yet.
  6. A computer follows a reconstruction algorithm (a step-by-step math recipe) to turn the raw data into image data: a grid (matrix) of tiny squares called pixels. Each pixel holds a CT number that says how dense that spot is.
  7. Stack many slices and you get a 3D block of tiny cubes called voxels. Other algorithms turn that block into 3D pictures.

The CT number scale (HU)

Every pixel gets a number in Hounsfield units (HU). Water is set to 0. Anything denser than water is positive, anything less dense is negative. You will use this scale in Part A.

  • −1000Air
  • −100Fat
  • 0Water
  • +40Soft tissue, organs
  • +200 to +400Vessels filled with contrast
  • +400 to +1000Bone

Higher number = denser = brighter on the image.

Words to know

The scanner

Dotted-underline word
Like this one. Tap any of them to see what it means.
CT (computed tomography)
An x-ray exam where a computer builds cross-section pictures ("slices") of the body from many x-ray measurements taken around the patient.
Gantry
The ring-shaped part of the scanner. The x-ray tube and detectors spin around inside it.
X-ray tube
The part that produces the x-ray beam. It circles the patient during the scan.
Detector
Sits opposite the tube and measures how much x-ray came out the other side of the patient.
Ray
One thin line of x-rays going from the tube, through the patient, to one detector element.
Helical (spiral) scanning
Scanning while the table keeps moving. The tube's path around the patient becomes a spiral (a helix), like the line on a spring.
Dose
How much radiation the patient receives. Lower dose is safer but the image gets grainier (more noise).

Data and images

Attenuation
How much the x-ray beam is weakened as it passes through something. Bone attenuates a lot, air almost nothing.
Ray sum
The total attenuation along one ray, meaning every value it passed through added together. The scanner only ever knows these totals, never the values inside.
Attenuation profile (projection)
All the ray sums measured from one angle, side by side. It looks like a row of bars, a kind of "shadow" of the body from that direction.
Raw data
The measurements straight from the detectors, before any image exists.
Sinogram
A picture of the raw data: every attenuation profile stacked as one row, one row per angle. It looks like wavy stripes, not like a body.
Image data
The finished picture after reconstruction: pixels with CT numbers.
Algorithm
A set of step-by-step rules for getting a specific output from a specific input. Like a recipe, but for a computer.
Reconstruction
The process of turning raw data into image data.
Matrix
The grid of rows and columns that makes up the image, for example 512 × 512 boxes.
Pixel
One box in the matrix (2D, flat).
Voxel
A pixel with thickness: one tiny cube of the patient's tissue. A pixel is what you see; a voxel is the piece of the body it stands for.
CT number / Hounsfield unit (HU)
The number stored in each pixel that tells how dense that tissue is. Water = 0, air = −1000, bone about +400 to +1000 or more.
Operator-selected pixel range
A range of CT numbers that the person running the scanner chooses, for example 200 to 1200 HU. Only values inside the range are used.
Axial slice
A cross-section image cut straight across the body, as if slicing bread.
Post-acquisition
Done after the scan is finished, using data already collected.

3D and exams

MPR (multiplanar reformation)
Re-arranging the stack of slices to view the body in other directions or in 3D, without scanning again.
Volume rendering
Showing the whole 3D block of voxels at once, partly see-through, so it looks like a real 3D object.
Contrast medium
A dye (usually iodine) injected into the patient so blood vessels and some organs show up bright.
CT angiography (CTA)
A CT scan of blood vessels, done with contrast medium.
Virtual colonoscopy
Using CT to "fly through" the inside of the colon on screen, instead of putting a camera inside it.

Image quality

Artifact
Anything on the image that is not really in the patient: a mistake made by the scanner or the math.
Star pattern
The artifact of simple back projection: spokes or a halo of lines around bright objects.
Streak artifact
Bright or dark lines across the image, often coming from metal, motion or too little x-ray.
Noise
Grainy, speckled look on the image. It gets worse when the dose is lower or when edges are sharpened.
Spatial resolution
How well the image shows tiny details and sharp edges as separate things.
Contrast resolution
How well the image shows tissues that are only slightly different in density.
CNR (contrast-to-noise ratio)
How clearly a structure stands out compared with the graininess around it. Higher is better.
Photon starvation
Too few x-rays reach the detector (for example through both shoulders), which causes streaks.

Processing words

Filter (kernel)
A math formula that changes the numbers in an attenuation profile, either to smooth them or to sharpen edges.
Convolution
Applying a filter to the raw data. Each value is changed based on its neighbours.
Deconvolution
The reverse process: returning pixel values to their original level.
Back projection
Taking each ray sum and spreading ("smearing") it back across the image along the path it came from, then adding everything up.
Interpolation
Estimating a missing value from the known values on either side of it.
Iteration
One round of a repeat. Iterative = done again and again, getting a bit better each time.
Fourier transformation
A math method that splits one complicated wave into simple waves of different frequencies.
Frequency
How fast a wave goes up and down. A high-frequency sound is a high-pitched sound.
GPU
A powerful computer chip built for heavy math. Iterative reconstruction needs it.

After the scan you have a stack of flat slices. Stacked together they make a 3D block of voxels, each with a CT number. To show the block as one 3D picture, the computer looks through it along many lines of sight, one for each dot on your screen. For each line it has to decide what to show. BMathematical algorithms

The scanner never takes a picture directly. It only collects numbers (raw data). A reconstruction algorithm is a set of rules or directions for getting a specific output from a specific input. Here the input is raw data and the output is an image. In the lecture's words, it solves a mathematical problem.

InputRaw data
ProcessSoftware (formula)
OutputImage data

Go through the topics in order. Each one builds on the one before it. At the end, the scanner bench puts them all together.

Back projection

The scanner never sees the values inside the patient. It only sees ray sums, the total of everything each ray passed through. Back projection works backwards: it takes each total, spreads it back along the path of its ray, and adds up all the spreads.

This is the worked example from the lecture. The object is a tiny 2 × 2 matrix with the values 1, 2, 3, 4. The scanner measures it from four directions. Press Next step to watch the matrix fill up.

Board points

  • Also called the summation or linear superposition method. Both names just mean "adding layers on top of each other".
  • Oldendorf (1961) and Kuhl and Edwards (1963)
  • Converts data from the attenuation profile into a matrix
  • Sums all back-projected images to form the CT image
  • Does not produce a sharp image. Not used in clinical CT.
  • Artifact: the typical star pattern
  • One back projection reveals little. Many back projections make the image look like the object.

Convolution

Back projection alone gives a blurry image. To fix that, the scanner changes the numbers first. A convolution filter is a math formula that changes an attenuation profile before it is back projected. Think of a photo filter: one kind smooths things out, another sharpens edges.

The bars below are one profile across a structure with sharp edges. Pick a filter and compare it with the dashed original.

Smoothing filterEdge-enhancing filter
Reduces the difference between adjacent (side-by-side) pixelsAccentuates (exaggerates) the difference between neighboring pixels
Low contrast resolution, low spatial resolutionHigh spatial resolution, high contrast resolution
Image smoothing: soft tissueEdge enhancement: bone
Low noise, more blurredLow blurring, high noise

Board points

  • A digital image processing technique
  • Applies a filter to an attenuation profile, changing pixel values with a formula
  • Used to minimize streak artifacts
  • Applied only to raw data, not image data
  • Deconvolution is the reverse: it returns pixel values to their original level

Memory trick: smooth = soft tissue, sharp = skeleton.

Filtered back projection

Convolution+Back projection=Filtered back projection

Filtered back projection (FBP) puts the last two topics together: filter each profile first, then back project it.

Below, one small dot is scanned. Left: plain back projection. Each smear runs right across the image, so spokes stick out of the dot. Right: each profile is filtered first. The filter adds small negative values beside every edge, and when those are smeared back they cancel the spokes. Slide the number of projections up and watch.

Board points

  • Also called the convolution method: the projection profile is convolved (filtered)
  • Advantage: removes the star-like blurring of simple back projection
  • Disadvantage: noise and streak artifacts

Fourier transformation

Fourier transformation splits one complicated wave into simple waves, each with its own frequency. Later, the simple waves can be added back together to rebuild the original.

The lecture compares it to hearing. A sound reaching your ear is one complicated wave. Your ear separates it into simple waves, and your brain puts them back together so you hear the original sound. Turn the waves on and off below, then listen.

Turn your volume down first.

  2. Ear

  4. Intensities
  5. Brain
  6. Rearranged
  7. Perception of the original sound

Board points

  • Used to reconstruct images of the patient's anatomy in MRI
  • Not used in modern CT scanners
  • Analogy: the act of hearing

Helical interpolation

In helical scanning the table keeps moving while the tube spins, so the tube draws a spiral around the patient instead of flat circles. That means no complete flat slice is ever measured.

Interpolation fills the gap: it estimates a missing value from the two known values on either side. Below, the spiral is "unrolled" flat, so it becomes slanted lines. Across is the tube angle, up is the table position. Move the slider to choose where the flat slice goes. For each angle, the scanner takes the real measurement just below the slice and the one just above it (one full 360° turn later) and estimates the value in between. This is 360° linear interpolation.

Board points

  • A mathematical method of creating missing data
  • Estimates an unknown value from known values
  • Linear interpolation: the unknown point falls on a straight line between two known points (A = 5, C = 15, so B = 10)
  • Used to straighten up the sectional image
  • A post-acquisition algorithm applied to the data set
  • The 1st algorithm used 360° linear interpolation. Disadvantage: prominent blurring.

Quick check · helical interpolation

Iterative reconstruction

Iterative means "repeated". Iterative reconstruction is guess and check:

  1. Start with a guess where every box in the matrix has the same value.
  2. Work out what the scanner would have measured if that guess were true.
  3. Compare it with what the scanner really measured.
  4. Correct the guess, then repeat until they match.

Same 1, 2, 3, 4 object as in back projection. Press Next step.

Board points

  • Starts by assuming all points in the matrix have the same value
  • Performed after obtaining all projection data sets
  • Advantages: reduces image noise (primary), low patient dose (primary), improves CNR, minimal streak artifacts (metal, motion, photon starvation)
  • Disadvantages: needs high computing power (GPUs), computationally intensive, slower reconstruction

  2. Change Method to Back projection and scan again. The image is blurry, the reason it is not used clinically.
  3. Pick Single dot and set projections to about 6. You will see the star pattern.
  4. Go back to Abdomen slice and Filtered back projection, then choose Low dose. The image gets grainy (noise). Switch between the two filters.

DLast-minute summary

Only the facts the board asks about. Read it the night before or print it.

MIP · Maximum intensity projection

  • Keeps the highest value pixel along each line
  • Simplest form of 3D imaging
  • Used in CT angiography

SSD · Shaded surface display

  • Computer-aided technique
  • A narrow range of values = the object; shows its surface
  • Bone imaging, virtual colonoscopy

SVD · Shaded volume display

  • Blends every voxel in the range
  • Looks very 3D (volume rendered)
  • Very sensitive to the operator-selected pixel range

Back projection

  • a.k.a. summation / linear superposition method
  • Oldendorf (1961), Kuhl and Edwards (1963)
  • Not sharp → star pattern → not used clinically
  • 2 × 2 example: (BP + P1 + P2 + P3 − 10) ÷ 3

Convolution

  • Filter applied to raw data only
  • Minimizes streak artifacts
  • Smoothing: soft tissue, low noise, more blur
  • Edge enhancing: bone, high spatial resolution, high noise
  • Deconvolution = reverse process

Filtered back projection

  • Convolution + back projection
  • Also called the convolution method
  • + Removes star-like blurring
  • − Noise and streak artifacts

Fourier transformation

  • Used in MRI
  • Not used in modern CT
  • Analogy: act of hearing

Helical interpolation

  • Creates missing data
  • Straightens up the sectional image
  • Post-acquisition
  • 1st algorithm: 360° linear → prominent blurring
  • A = 5, C = 15 → B = 10

Iterative reconstruction

  • Starts with all points the same value
  • Done after all projection data is collected
  • + Less noise, low dose (primary), better CNR, fewer streaks (metal, motion, photon starvation)
  • − Needs GPUs, computationally intensive, slower
