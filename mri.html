Chapter I

Overview

What MRI is, where it sits on the electromagnetic spectrum, the four basic steps, what it does well and badly, and the people who built it.

1.1

Definition and the electromagnetic spectrum

Magnetic resonance imaging (once called nuclear magnetic resonance, NMR) creates mathematically reconstructed 3D images of structures through the interaction of a magnetic field and radio waves with tissue. No x-rays are used.

Where MRI sits on the spectrum

Board points

  • Old name: nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
  • Uses a magnetic field (MF) and radiofrequency (RF) waves
  • Magnetic field range: 0.1–3.0 T
  • RF range: 10–300 MHz
  • Images are mathematically reconstructed 3D images
1.2

Basic steps of MRI

  1. Step 1Patient is placed inside the magnetprotons line up with the field
  2. Step 2RF pulse is sent inprotons absorb energy and tip over
  3. Step 3RF pulse is turned offprotons relax back
  4. Step 4Patient emits a signalpicked up by the coil, made into an image

Chapter II plays these four steps out proton by proton in the spin lab.

1.4

Brief history

1946 · Felix Bloch

✦

Discovered the properties of magnetic resonance: nuclei absorb radio-wave energy at certain distinct frequencies. Described NMR in solids and water. The nucleus has a magnetic field because it spins. Called the Father of MRI in the lecture.

1946 · Edward Purcell

✦

Described NMR in solids, at the same time as Bloch. 1952: Bloch and Purcell shared the Nobel Prize in Physics.

1960s–1976 · Raymond Damadian

✦

Showed that malignant tissue has a different NMR spectrum and different PD, T1 and T2 from normal tissue. 1974: first NMR image of a rat tumor. 1976: first body image, which took 4 hours.

1973 · Paul Lauterbur

✦

NMR chemist. Developed the first imaging method using NMR: zeugmatography ("a whole bunch of MF whizzing and buzzing around"). Published the first cross-sectional NMR image: two water-filled capillary tubes.

Peter Mansfield

✦

Developed echo planar imaging (EPI): a fast pulse sequence that makes images within a few seconds. Used in functional neuroimaging.

2003 · Lauterbur & Mansfield

✦

Shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Prof's tip

Two Nobel pairs: Bloch + Purcell = Physics, 1952. Lauterbur + Mansfield = Medicine, 2003. Match the person to the "first": Damadian = rat tumor and first body image, Lauterbur = capillary tubes and zeugmatography, Mansfield = EPI.

Chapter II

Physical principles

Why a spinning proton is a tiny magnet, how millions of them add up to one vector, and what happens to that vector when the magnet and the RF pulse go to work.

  • Motion in the atom
  • MR active nuclei
  • Moment, NMV & axes

2.1

Motion within the atom: Oersted and Faraday

Oersted: current makes a magnetic field

No current, no field. A spinning charged particle (the proton's primary spin) is a tiny loop of current, so it induces a magnetic field: a magnetic moment, or magnetic dipole.

Faraday: a moving field makes current

This is how the signal is caught: the protons' moving magnetization induces a current in the receiver coil.

Primary spinThe nucleus spinning on its own axis. Makes the magnetic moment.
Secondary spin = precessionThe spinning nucleus wobbling around the main field, like a spinning top.
2.2

MR active nuclei and hydrogen

MR active nuclei have an odd number of protons (Z) and an odd atomic mass number (A). They act like tiny bar magnets: their spins don't cancel each other out, so the nucleus spins and induces a field. With even Z and A, the spins cancel out: no net spin, no magnetic field.

Hydrogen nucleus

  • Most commonly used MR active nucleus
  • A single proton and no neutron
  • Abundant in the body: fat and water
  • Gives a large magnetic moment
  • Odd Z + odd A → spins don't cancel → nucleus spins
  • Even Z + even A → spins cancel → no net spin
2.3

Magnetic moment, net magnetization and the coordinate system

Magnetic moment

  • A vector: has size and direction, drawn as an arrow
  • Vector direction = direction of the magnetic moment
  • Vector length = size of the magnetic moment
  • Magnetic dipole: a field with its own north and south pole

Net magnetization (M)

  • The sum of the individual magnetic moments
  • No external MF: moments point every way, M = 0
  • In an external MF: they line up, M = 1

Net magnetization vector (NMV)

  • The net magnetism of the patient, used to get a signal
  • Made by the excess hydrogen nuclei aligned with B0
  • Longitudinal (Mz): parallel to B0, cannot be measured
  • Transverse (Mxy): after a 90° RF pulse, perpendicular to B0, can be measured

The coordinate system

Z-axis: the axis of B0, along the patient's long axis (Mz). XY-axes: across the patient, where the receiver coil sits (Mxy).

2.4

MRI signal production

Four stages: the patient is put in B0, an RF pulse is applied, the RF is turned off, and the patient emits the signal. Step through them and watch the protons and the NMV.

Lab

The spin lab

RF pulse frequency

During exposure to B0

2
  • Alignment (polarization): moments line up with B0. Spin up = parallel, low energy. Spin down = antiparallel, high energy. A few more go spin up, so the patient becomes a magnet: Mz = 1, Mxy = 0 (equilibrium)
  • Precession: the wobble of spinning protons around B0, tracing a cone
  • B0 = the main magnetic field, measured in tesla (T)

Larmor frequency

ω

The precession frequency. It depends on the strength of B0.

ω₀ = γ × B0
γ (hydrogen) = 42.6 MHz/T (gyromagnetic ratio)
1.0 T → 42.6 MHz · 1.5 T → 63.9 MHz · 3.0 T → 127.8 MHz

Hydrogen imaging at 1 T: the RF coil is tuned to about 42 MHz.

During the RF pulse

3
  • The RF pulse disturbs the protons only if RF frequency = Larmor frequency. It has a "whip-like" action
  • Resonance: RF energy absorption, only at the Larmor frequency
  • Phase coherence: protons precess in phase in the xy plane, where the most signal is made
  • Phase = the position of a moment on its circular path
  • NMV flipped 90°: Mz decreases, Mxy increases, more high-energy spin-down nuclei. Mz = 0, Mxy = 1: maximum signal (excited state)

After the RF pulse

4
  • Relaxation: protons return to equilibrium (T1) and phase coherence fades (T2)
  • The FID signal is emitted: Mz = 1, Mxy = 0 again (relaxed state)

2.5

Relaxation, T1, T2 and the FID

Relaxation is the return of excited nuclei to their normal state by releasing energy (as heat). The relaxation time is how long that takes. Relaxation makes the signal weaker (low SNR, high CNR), and T1 and T2 are the most important factors for tissue contrast.

T1: longitudinal, spin-lattice

z
  • Time for 63% of Mz to recover
  • Happens on the z-axis: nuclei flip back into line with B0
  • Energy goes to the surroundings, the lattice
  • Depends on surroundings, tissue composition, structure

T2: transverse, spin-spin

xy
  • Time for Mxy to decay by 63% (to 37%)
  • Happens in the xy plane: nuclei go out of phase
  • Energy passes between spins: spin-spin
  • Depends on external and internal MF inhomogeneity

Board points

  • T1 is normally longer than T2, about 2–10 times
  • T1 range: 300–2000 ms
  • T2 range: 30–150 ms
  • FID (free induction decay): the primary MRI signal. Loss of signal from relaxation shortly after the RF pulse is removed: the induced signal in the coil starts to fall (the T2* curve)
  • Fat: shortest T1. CSF: longest T1 and T2
Prof's tip

Remember the two numbers: T1 = 63% recovery, T2 = 63% decay (37% left). And the two nicknames: T1 = spin-lattice, T2 = spin-spin.

Primary and secondary electromagnetic assemblies

Primary assembly

  • The primary magnetic coils
  • Not visible: in plastic housing, deep in the cryostat

Secondary assembly

  • The secondary magnetic coils, covered by thick plastic
  • Three sets: RF coil, gradient coils, shim coils
3.4

RF coils

The RF coil, a.k.a. the integrated body coil, is a separate, removable assembly kept at room temperature (not cryogenic) and sits nearest the patient. It sends the RF pulse at the hydrogen resonance frequency, making a field perpendicular (90°) to B0. Local coils are shaped for the body part: brain and neck, neck and spine, shoulder, chest-abdomen-pelvis.

Volume coil

  • Transceiver: transmits RF and receives the signal
  • Mostly quadrature coils (two pairs)
  • Leg, knee, foot, head
  • Images large areas, lower SNR

Surface coil

  • Used to improve SNR, shaped to fit one area
  • Receives signal only up to the coil's edge: superficial
  • The body coil transmits the RF
  • Deep structures: volume, phased array or a local coil (e.g. endorectal)

Linear phased array

  • Multiple coils and receivers
  • Signals combined into one image: better SNR and coverage
  • Used for the spine

Volume phased array

  • Coils around the volume, each filling its own K-space line: 4 coils = 4 lines at a time
  • Parallel imaging: 2–32 channels, extra hardware and software
  • Produces aliasing, removed by software
  • Shorter scan time, higher resolution

Board points

  • RF coil = integrated body coil, room temperature, nearest the patient
  • RF field is 90° to B0
  • Volume coil: transmit + receive, large area, low SNR
  • Surface coil: receive only, high SNR, superficial
  • Phased array: more SNR + coverage · parallel imaging: faster
3.5

Gradient and shim coils

Gradient coils

x y z
  • Large conductors that make a pulsed gradient MF, altering B0
  • Switched on and off very fast: the Lorentz force on the conductors causes the thumping sound
  • Used for slice selection and signal localization (x, y, z axes)
  • Closest to the patient aperture, room temperature

Shim coils

≈
  • Make the MF more homogeneous: shimming
  • Between the gradient coils and the primary assembly
  • Common in systems of 3 T or higher
  • Early systems: room temperature. Advanced: in the cryostat
  • Sags (inhomogeneities) come from unevenly spaced loops. Imaging needs 10 ppm homogeneity

Prof's tip

Fat has a short T1 (about 260 ms, fast recovery, strong signal) so it is bright on T1. Water has a long T1 (about 3500 ms) so it is dark on T1, but its long T2 makes it bright on T2.

SNR vs CNR

  • Short TR: low SNR, high CNR (high T1 contrast)
  • Long TR: high SNR, low CNR

4.4

Extrinsic parameters: TI, flip angle and b value

Extrinsic parameters can be controlled by the RT: repetition time (TR), echo time (TE), inversion time (TI), flip angle and b value.

Lab

Inversion time: null the fat or the CSF

TI is the time between the 180° inversion pulse and the 90° pulse. After the 180° pulse, Mz starts upside down and recovers. Whatever crosses zero at the moment of the 90° pulse gives no signal.

Inversion time (TI)

  • Main weighting control in inversion recovery
  • Suppresses CSF or fat
  • Short TI 100–180 ms: STIR (dark fat)
  • Long TI 1700–2200 ms: FLAIR (dark water)

Flip angle (FA)

  • a.k.a. tip angle: how far the NMV is moved by the RF pulse
  • 90° and 180° are the most common
  • < 90° = alpha (α) pulse, for fast imaging (10–30°)
  • 90° abolishes Mz: no useful signal if the next pulse comes after a very short TR. Large FA → T1WI
  • Small FA leaves part of Mz, so short TR still works. Small FA → T2WI and PDWI

b value

  • The strength and duration of the diffusion gradients
  • Controls contrast in a DWI sequence
  • In s/mm², range 500–1000
  • High b exaggerates differences in ADC, and senses slower-moving water and smaller diffusion distances

Chapter V

Pulse sequences

Why the spins need rephasing, the two ways to do it, and the RF pulse pattern of each basic sequence.

5.1

MRI pulse sequences

A pulse sequence is a series of RF pulses, gradient applications and intervening time periods.

Right after the RF pulse is removed, the spins dephase and the signal falls to the null point. Without a sequence to rephase them and compensate for field inhomogeneities, there is not enough signal to make an image. The sequence also controls image weighting.

Main purpose

  • Rephase spins
  • Remove inhomogeneity effects
  • Produce a signal, the echo
  • Let TR and TE be changed for different contrast

Spins are rephased by

  • A 180° RF pulse (spin echo family)
  • A gradient (gradient echo)

Board points

  • Spin echo: 90°–180°
  • Fast spin echo: 90° then many 180°s
  • Saturation recovery / partial saturation: 90°–90°–90°
  • Inversion recovery: 180°–90°
  • Gradient echo: α–α–α (less than 90°)
Visual

The RF pulse pattern of each sequence

Tall bar = 180°, medium = 90°, short = α. The orange 180° pulses are the rephasing pulses. Gradient echo has none: it rephases with a gradient.

Safety and shielding

6.1

The three MRI energy fields

Safety matters because the field interacts with metal objects and tissues. Patients and staff nearby are exposed to the static, gradient and RF magnetic fields.

Static field (B0)

Interaction: polarization
  • The best-recognized field, in tesla
  • Clinical maximum 8 T; 3 T and below is common
  • Always on, 24/7. The fringe field can reach several meters past the room
  • Bioeffects: taller ECG T-wave, heating, fatigue, headaches, hypotension, irritability

Gradient field (Bx, By, Bz)

Interaction: induced current
  • Time-varying, only during scanning
  • Exposure limited to patients and relatives in the room
  • Induces current in nerves, vessels and muscles (the body is a conductor)
  • Bioeffects: light flashes in the eyes, changes in cell biochemistry and fracture union, mild skin sensations, involuntary muscle contractions, cardiac arrhythmias

RF field (B1)

Interaction: thermal heating
  • Responsible for significant burn hazards
  • Bioeffect: tissue heating, which raises blood pressure and heart rate

Safety concerns associated with MRI

  1. Potential hazard of projectiles
  2. Electrical interference with implants
  3. Torquing of certain metallic objects
  4. Local heating of tissues and metallic objects
  5. Electrical interference with normal function of nerve cells and muscle fibers
6.2

Projectiles and the gauss lines

The projectile hazard comes from the fringe field: a static field around the magnet, measured in gauss (G), falling off as 1/d³ from the bore. At 1.5 T, a metal object 3 ft away is pulled at 10 × gravity; at 7 ft, about 1 × gravity.

Lab

How close can it go?

Board points

  • 50 G line: no oxygen tanks, IV pumps, wheelchairs, carts, patient monitors
  • 10 G line: no magnetic tapes, credit cards, analog watches
  • 5 G line: no pacemakers
  • Room needs warning posters and door security
  • Code blue: take the patient out of the scan room first
6.3

Implants, torquing, heating and nerve stimulation

Electrical interference with implants

  • Can damage electronic parts and affect cardiac pacemakers
  • RF pulses induce voltage in pacemaker leads

Contraindications to MRI

  • Pacemakers
  • Ferromagnetic aneurysm clips
  • Metallic fragment in the eye
  • Cochlear implants
  • Neurostimulators
  • Implanted drug infusion pumps
  • Bone growth stimulators
  • Ferromagnetic GI surgical clips

Torquing

  • A twisting movement of ferromagnetic objects (surgical clips) in the static field
  • Can damage tissue around the surgical site
  • Screen patients carefully: a conventional radiograph before MRI

Nerve and muscle stimulation

  • From currents induced by rapidly changing gradient fields
  • Gradient strength: 10–40 mT/m
  • 3 T/s induces about 3 µA/cm², enough for involuntary contraction and cardiac fibrillation in experimental animals

Local heating and SAR

W/kg
  • RF pulses heat tissue. Heating depends on number of slices, FA, NSA, TR and tissue type
  • One reason pregnant women are not routinely scanned (fetal temperature). 20 W/kg caused abnormalities in the offspring of pregnant animals
  • SAR (specific absorption rate) measures tissue heating, in watts per kilogram. 1–4 W/kg is high risk for elderly and dehydrated patients
  • Whole-body limits: 1.5 T → 3.5 W/kg, 3.0 T → 3.0 W/kg, each over 15 minutes
  • The RT must enter the patient's weight. SAR is highest with sequences that use many 180° pulses
6.4

Zoning of an MRI suite

Zones control access to the scanner with barriers that protect patients and staff. Recommended by the American College of Radiology (ACR) White Paper on MR safety. Each entrance is clearly marked.

Zone IEverything outside the MR environment, freely open to the public. No risk. Waiting area, corridor.
Zone IIPatients and visitors supervised by MR staff. The bridge between Zone I and III/IV. Screening and history taking. Reception, preparation area.
Zone IIIRestricted to MR personnel, marked as hazardous. Exclusion zone set by the 5 gauss line; no pacemakers or defibrillators. Pass-key lock. Computer room, control room.
Zone IVThe scanner room: most potential risk. Warning light and signs ("The magnet is ALWAYS on"). Entrance always visible to MR staff.
6.5

MRI quenching

  1. NormalCoils near absolute zerosuperconducting, field on
  2. QuenchSudden loss of the coldcoils become resistive
  3. ResultField is losthelium boils off very fast
  • The helium-venting system carries the helium outside the building
  • Started by pressing the quench button, or happens by accident
  • Press it only if someone in the scan room is pinned to the magnet by a projectile that can't be removed by hand

Board points

  • Quench = sudden loss of superconductivity
  • Coils become resistive, field is lost
  • Helium escapes rapidly
  • Quench button: person pinned by a projectile
6.6

MRI shielding

Passive shielding

Fe
  • Iron in the room walls or around the magnet reduces the fringe field
  • Rarely used: heavy (250 tons at 7 T) and expensive

Active shielding

✦
  • The standard today
  • A second set of superconducting coils outside the primary ones, current flowing the opposite way
  • Cuts the fringe field (0.5 mT kept within 4 m), improves homogeneity
  • Magnets shortened from 2 m to 1.5 m. Expensive

Active shielded gradient

  • Self-shielding gradient coils just outside the imaging gradients
  • Make eddy currents that cancel the eddy currents of the main gradient coils

Faraday cage

  • Metal sheets or mesh around the room
  • Keeps outside RF out of the scan room (stops zipper artifacts)

  • Permanent: open, 0.1–0.3 T, iron yoke, no shim coil (pole faces)
  • Earth 0.00005 T

IIICoils

  • Patient outward: RF → gradient → shim → primary
  • RF = integrated body coil, 90° to B0, room temp
  • Volume: transceiver, low SNR · surface: high SNR, superficial
  • Gradient: Lorentz force → thump · slice selection, localization
  • Shim: homogeneity 10 ppm, ≥ 3 T

IVWeighting

  • T1WI: short TR, short TE · fat bright, CSF dark
  • T2WI: long TR, long TE · CSF bright
  • PDWI: long TR, short TE
  • Short TR + long TE = very weak
  • T2* < T2 (B0 inhomogeneity)

IVExtrinsic

  • STIR: TI 100–180 ms, dark fat
  • FLAIR: TI 1700–2200 ms, dark CSF
  • FA < 90° = α, 10–30°, fast imaging · large FA T1WI
  • b value 500–1000 s/mm² · stroke bright on DWI, dark on ADC

VPulse sequences

  • RF pulses + gradients + time periods
  • Rephase with 180° RF or gradient
  • SE 90–180 · FSE 90–180–180… · SR 90–90 · IR 180–90 · GRE α–α

VISafety

  • B0 → polarization · gradient → induced current · RF → heating
  • 50 G O₂ tanks · 10 G cards, watches · 5 G pacemakers
  • Fringe ∝ 1/d³ · code blue: patient out first
  • SAR W/kg: 1.5 T 3.5, 3 T 3.0 (15 min)

VIZones, quench, shields

  • Zone I public · II screening · III control, 5 G · IV magnet
  • Quench: loss of superconductivity, helium out · button = person pinned
  • Passive iron (rare) · active (standard) · self-shielded gradient · Faraday cage (RF)

VIIArtifacts

  • Patient: motion (ghost/smear) · chemical shift (fat–water, kidneys) · misregistration (india ink, Dixon) · susceptibility (metal, air–bone, GRE/EPI)
  • System: distortion (FOV edge) · zipper (RF, Faraday cage) · magic angle (55°, short TE)
  • User: truncation (rings) · wrap-around (small FOV) · cross-talk (overlap)
Appendix

Words to know

Every dotted word on the page links here. Plain-language definitions first, board wording in the chapters.

The basics

MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)
A scan that uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not x-rays, to make pictures of the inside of the body. Especially good for soft tissue.
Tesla (T)
The unit of magnetic field strength. Clinical scanners are 0.1 to 3 T. 1 T = 10,000 gauss.
Gauss (G)
A smaller unit of magnetic field, used for the weak fringe field around the magnet (the 5 G line, for example).
RF (radiofrequency) pulse
A short burst of radio waves sent into the patient to tip the protons over.
Magnetic moment
The tiny magnet made by one spinning proton, drawn as an arrow showing its strength and direction.
NMV (net magnetization vector)
All the protons' tiny magnets added together into one arrow: the patient's overall magnetism.

Spins and signal

Precession
The wobble of a spinning proton around the main field, like a spinning top that leans and circles.
Larmor frequency
How fast the protons precess. It grows with the field: 42.6 MHz for every tesla.
Resonance
Taking in energy only when it arrives at exactly the right frequency, like pushing a swing in time with it.
Phase coherence
All the protons pointing the same way at the same moment as they precess. That is when the signal is strongest.
Relaxation
Protons settling back to normal after the RF pulse, giving off the energy they absorbed.
FID (free induction decay)
The signal the coil picks up right after the RF pulse. It fades quickly as the protons fall out of step.

Scan settings

TR (repetition time)
The time from one RF pulse to the next. Controls T1 contrast.
TE (echo time)
The time from the RF pulse to reading the signal. Controls T2 contrast.
TI (inversion time)
In inversion recovery, the wait between the 180° pulse and the 90° pulse. Chooses which tissue goes black.
Flip angle
How far the RF pulse tips the NMV over, in degrees.
SNR / CNR
Signal-to-noise ratio: how strong the signal is compared with graininess. Contrast-to-noise ratio: how well tissues stand apart.
Pulse sequence
The recipe of RF pulses, gradients and waiting times that makes one kind of image.

Safety and hardware

Fringe field
The part of the magnet's field that leaks out around it. It is what pulls metal objects in.
SAR (specific absorption rate)
How much RF heat the body takes in, in watts per kilogram of body weight.
Quench
The magnet suddenly warming and losing its field, with the liquid helium boiling away.
Cryogen
A liquefied gas, like helium, that keeps the magnet coils extremely cold.
K-space
The grid where the raw signal is stored line by line before it is turned into an image.
Bandwidth
The range of frequencies the scanner listens to. Wider bandwidth means less fat–water shift and less susceptibility distortion.
